# EBP and Research Process Guide

This guide provides an organized reference map and a "cheat sheet" for applying evidence-based practice and the scientific research process to clinical projects.

---

## Organized Reference Outline

- **Scientific research process (end-to-end workflow)**
  - **Step 1: Formulate the research question + hypothesis**
    - Where questions come from (practice, policy, current issues, literature, theory)
    - “Good question” screen: FINER (feasible, interesting, novel, ethical, relevant) + feasibility constraints (resources/data/access)
    - Clinical question framing: PICOTS (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, Timeline, Setting)
    - Outcome framing: ECHO outcomes (Economic, Clinical, Humanistic)
    - Descriptive vs analytical research; when hypotheses are/aren’t used
  - **Step 2: Develop the research plan (design + methods + approvals)**
    - Regulatory/IRB requirements before implementation
    - Study designs: experimental vs observational (e.g., RCTs vs cohort/cross-sectional) and why strength differs (bias/confounding)
    - Methods: primary vs secondary data; prospective vs retrospective considerations; reliability/validity goals
  - **Step 3: Data collection, management, and analysis**
    - Record/coding at the unit-of-analysis level; data integrity matters
    - Descriptive vs inferential statistics; selecting tests based on question/design/methods
    - Reporting significance and uncertainty (e.g., p-values, confidence intervals)
  - **Step 4: Communicate findings (research report/poster)**
    - IMRaD structure (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion) and abstracts/posters as dissemination formats
- **Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM)**
  - Definition: integrating research evidence with clinical expertise and patient values
  - Core EBM steps: ask, find, appraise, apply
  - Quality aims that motivate evidence-based care (safe/effective/patient-centered/timely/efficient/equitable)
- **Implementation science (closing the “evidence → practice” gap)**
  - Why adoption is delayed; factors spanning context, organization, professionals, and the intervention itself
  - Implementation science as the study of methods to promote uptake of evidence-based practices into routine care
- **Professional education / research–practice continuum**
  - Continuum from basic → applied → clinical → translational research, plus infrastructure/programs that support it
  - Networks and collaborative models (e.g., practice-based research networks) supporting real-world research and translation

## Topic Cheat-Sheet

| Topic | What it is | How it should be used |
|---|---|---|
| **Scientific research process** | A staged workflow from question/hypothesis → plan/design → data/analysis → reporting results. | Use it as your project checklist so you don’t skip fundamentals (question quality, design fit, analysis plan, dissemination). |
| **FINER** | A set of criteria to judge whether a research question is feasible, interesting, novel, ethical, and relevant. | Use it to quickly “kill or refine” weak topics before investing time in protocol writing or data extraction. |
| **PICOTS** | A structure to specify Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, Timeline, and Setting for a clinical research question. | Use it to translate a vague idea into a protocol-ready question and to align inclusion criteria, endpoints, follow-up window, and setting. |
| **PICO (EBM framing)** | A simplified question structure emphasizing patient/problem, intervention, comparator, outcome. | Use it for rapid evidence searching and bedside/clinical decision support, especially when timeline/setting are implicit. |
| **ECHO outcomes** | A way to group outcomes into Economic, Clinical, and Humanistic categories. | Use it to ensure evaluation isn’t “A1c only” (or one metric only) and to plan balanced outcome measurement for stakeholders. |
| **Descriptive vs analytical research** | Descriptive explores characteristics (e.g., prevalence), while analytical evaluates relationships between variables/interventions and outcomes. | Use this to decide whether you need a hypothesis/causal design (analytical) or a measurement/estimation approach (descriptive). |
| **Experimental vs observational designs** | Experimental (e.g., randomized trials) tests interventions with stronger causal inference; observational (cohort/cross-sectional) estimates associations and is more vulnerable to bias/confounding. | Use this to pick the strongest feasible design, then proactively plan controls for confounding/bias if observational. |
| **Primary vs secondary data** | Primary data are collected specifically for the study (often prospectively); secondary data come from existing sources like charts/claims (often retrospective). | Use this to set expectations for data quality, missingness, measurement control, and timeline/cost; then tailor QA and analysis accordingly. |
| **Reliability & validity** | Reliability = consistency; validity = accuracy of measurement. | Use these as measurement requirements when selecting instruments, defining variables, and writing your data dictionary/abstraction rules. |
| **Descriptive vs inferential statistics** | Descriptive summarizes the sample; inferential draws conclusions using statistical tests and uncertainty estimates. | Use descriptive stats to understand data and check assumptions; use inferential stats to answer the primary research question. |
| **IMRaD** | Standard research report structure: Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion; often paired with structured abstracts. | Use it as your writing template early—draft Methods/Analysis first so the study is reproducible and coherent before results exist. |
| **Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM)** | Practice approach combining best evidence with clinician expertise and patient values to support patient-centered decisions. | Use it to guide clinical decisions and policy discussions: formulate the question, find/appraise evidence, then apply with patient preferences. |
| **Implementation science** | Field focused on methods to increase routine uptake of evidence-based practices; addresses barriers at context/organization/professional/intervention levels. | Use it when you already know “what works” but adoption is inconsistent—design workflows, incentives, training, and evaluation for uptake. |
| **Research–practice continuum** | A view of biomedical work spanning basic discovery → applied development → clinical evaluation → translation into practice. | Use it to position your project (where it lives on the continuum) and choose partners, endpoints, and dissemination targets accordingly. |

## How to Use This for Your Work

Start by writing a one‑sentence PICOTS question and run it through FINER; if it fails “feasible” or “relevant,” revise before doing anything else. Next, pick a design (experimental if possible; otherwise observational with explicit bias/confounding mitigation) and decide whether data will be primary/prospective or secondary/retrospective so the methods and limitations are honest from day one. Then predefine outcomes (ECHO is a solid checklist), write your analysis plan, and draft the IMRaD skeleton early so data collection and reporting stay aligned.
